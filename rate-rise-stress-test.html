METHODOLOGY

What this stress test is—and is not—doing.

Payment-stress method

  1. Model the scheduled payment from the current balance, rate, remaining term and repayment cadence, unless you explicitly use a current-payment override.
  2. For a future reset, project the balance forward using that current scheduled payment; do not amortize the loan over only the pre-reset period.
  3. At the reset point, calculate a same-rate reference payment over the remaining term and a stressed-rate payment over that same term.
  4. The difference between those two payments is the pure rate effect. The stressed payment is also compared separately with what you actually pay today.
  5. Weekly and every-two-weeks payments are converted to monthly equivalents before optional household-budget comparison.
  6. If household figures are entered, the zero-buffer rate is solved mathematically between 0% and 50%. It is not called a safe or affordable rate.

Important boundaries

  • No future-rate forecast or central-bank probability.
  • No lender affordability, DTI, eligibility or credit assessment.
  • No term extension or refinance-fee comparison inside MDC1C.
  • US fixed-rate borrowers do not automatically get a higher payment just because market rates rise; use this for an ARM adjustment, renewal/reset, refinance or other future-rate scenario that actually changes your loan rate.
  • Income and expenses are held constant if you model a future reset.

FAQ

Questions about mortgage payment stress

Does a 1% rate rise mean my payment rises by 1%?

No. A move from 6% to 7% is a one percentage-point increase in the annual rate, but the payment change depends on the balance, remaining term, repayment frequency and interest convention.

Why project the balance to a future renewal or reset date?

If the new rate applies later, the balance should usually be lower by then. Applying the future rate to today's balance would overstate the amount still owed at the modeled reset point.

Is the modeled zero-buffer rate my maximum affordable rate?

No. It is only the mathematical rate at which the entered monthly take-home income minus entered essential outgoings equals the modeled monthly-equivalent mortgage payment. It is not a lender rule or a recommendation.

Can I use this for a fixed-rate US mortgage?

Only when the loan rate can actually change—for example an ARM adjustment, future refinance or another contractual reset. A typical fully fixed-rate mortgage payment does not automatically change when market rates rise.
