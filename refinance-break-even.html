HOW THE BREAK-EVEN MODEL WORKS

Compare borrowing cost, not just payments.

Principal repayment moves debt from outstanding to repaid; it is not itself a borrowing cost. Carrowmont compares cumulative interest plus entered switching costs and cashback.

Calculation sequence

  1. Model the current loan from balance, rate, remaining term and repayment frequency.
  2. Model the new rate over the same remaining term using the same principal. This isolates the rate effect.
  3. Model the proposed refinance using the lender's proposed term and any financed switching fees.
  4. At each repayment period, compare current-loan interest with refinance interest plus net switching cost.
  5. Call break-even only when the advantage becomes non-negative and does not turn negative again through the full modeled horizon.
  6. If you enter a holding period, show the modeled saving or additional cost at that checkpoint separately from lifetime economics.

Important limitations

  • No lender ranking, eligibility decision or rate feed.
  • No automatic country penalty, tax or legal-rule calculation.
  • V1 holds the entered rates constant and uses one repayment frequency for both loans.
  • Property value, selling costs, future refinancing and variable-rate paths are not modeled.

FAQ

Questions about refinance break-even

Why show a new-rate payment using my old remaining term?

It isolates the rate effect. If the lender also stretches the term, the actual proposed payment can fall for two different reasons: the rate and the longer repayment horizon.

Why are financed fees still counted as a cost?

Financing changes when the fee is paid, not whether it exists. The fee is added to the new principal and also creates additional interest.

Why can a refinance cross break-even and later become unfavorable?

A long term can produce early interest savings but accumulate more interest later. Carrowmont therefore uses a durable break-even that remains non-negative through the full modeled comparison horizon.

Does this tell me whether I should refinance?

No. It is an educational borrowing-cost comparison under the assumptions you enter. Affordability, eligibility, lender terms, taxes, legal rules and future rate changes require separate consideration.
